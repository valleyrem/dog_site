{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Raising a puppy{% endblocktranslate %}

{% include 'woof/includes/guide_disclaimer.html' %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}What to expect during puppyhood{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Puppyhood is the earliest and most formative stage of a dog’s life. During this period, dogs rapidly develop habits, reactions, confidence, and social understanding of the world around them.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Raising a puppy is not only about care, but about helping the dog adapt safely and gradually to human environments, routines, sounds, and interactions.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}The first days at home{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Moving into a new home can be stressful for a puppy. New smells, unfamiliar people, and separation from littermates create a major environmental change.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}The first days should remain calm and predictable. A quiet resting space and stable routine help the puppy adapt more comfortably.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}A sample first-week schedule{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}The first week is for adaptation, not education. A simple rhythm works well: wake up and go outside immediately, short play, breakfast, a long calm rest, a short walk, rest again, an evening walk, dinner, and quiet time before bed.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Keep visitors, trips and noise to a minimum during this week. The puppy only needs to learn two things: where its place is, and that the household is predictable.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}House training: a practical routine{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Take the puppy outside right after waking up, after every meal, after play, and before sleep. Puppies of eight to ten weeks usually need to go every two to three hours; older puppies can wait longer.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Choose one spot and use the same phrase each time. Praise or treat the puppy immediately after it finishes — the reward must come within a second or two to stay connected with the action.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Accidents are part of the process. Clean the spot with an enzyme cleaner so the smell does not invite a repeat; punishment after the fact only teaches the puppy to hide from you while eliminating.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Early socialization{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Early socialization introduces the puppy to different environments, sounds, surfaces, people, and situations. Positive early experiences help build confidence and adaptability later in life.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Socialization should happen gradually and without overwhelming the puppy. Too much stimulation at once can create stress instead of confidence.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}A socialization checklist{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Make a short list and work through it slowly: different surfaces (grass, pavement, metal grates, laminate flooring), common sounds (vacuum cleaner, traffic, doorbell, fireworks recordings at low volume), and calm people of different ages and appearances.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Pair each new experience with something the puppy likes — food, play, or attention — and end the session before the puppy becomes overwhelmed. Three short positive encounters teach more than one long overwhelming one.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}How much walking is right{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}A common rule of thumb for growing puppies: about five minutes of structured walking per month of age, twice a day. A three-month-old therefore gets roughly fifteen minutes per walk, plus free play at home.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Surfaces, sounds and slow exploration matter more than distance. Avoid forced long marches, excessive stairs, and running beside a bicycle while the joints are still forming.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Sleep and daily rhythm{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Puppies sleep significantly more than adult dogs and require frequent rest periods. Overtired puppies often become more restless, reactive, or difficult to calm.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}A stable daily rhythm helps regulate energy and improves adaptation to home life.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Where the puppy sleeps{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Choose one quiet corner — not a corridor and not a draughty spot. A bed or a crate of the right size: the puppy should be able to lie fully stretched out and turn around comfortably.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}The first nights are often noisy. A warm hot-water bottle and a soft ticking clock can mimic the littermates; decide in advance whether the puppy may sleep in your bed, and keep that rule consistent from day one.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Teething and chewing phase{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}During teething, puppies naturally explore objects through chewing. This stage is temporary but requires patience and proper management of the environment.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Safe chew toys and supervision help redirect destructive chewing behavior.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Biting and bite inhibition{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Puppies explore and play with their mouths, so biting is normal. Teach bite inhibition by stopping the game the exact moment teeth touch skin: stand up, turn away, wait a few seconds, then resume play.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Never use your hand as a toy. If the puppy bites too hard, yelp once, go quiet and pause the game; over a few weeks the bite pressure softens naturally.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Building confidence{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Confidence develops through repeated positive experiences and predictable routines. Gentle exposure to new environments helps puppies learn that unfamiliar situations are safe.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Fearful reactions during puppyhood are normal and should be handled calmly rather than punished.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Teaching to be alone{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Being alone is learned, not innate. Start with tiny departures: stand up, step out for three seconds, come back — and stay boring. Repeat until the puppy stays completely calm.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Then increase the time gradually: a few seconds, then a minute, then several minutes. Leave a predictable cue — a long-lasting chew or a stuffed toy — and never make arrivals or departures emotional events.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Puppy-proofing your home{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Get down to the puppy's eye level and remove what it can reach: cables, small objects, shoes, cleaning products, toxic plants and dangling curtains. Closed doors beat constant supervision.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Secure the trash bin, keep bathroom doors closed, and use baby gates to limit the first weeks to one or two rooms. The smaller the free space, the fewer mistakes everyone has to correct.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}First veterinary visits{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Book a check-up in the first days after moving in. The vet will examine the puppy, discuss the timing of vaccinations and deworming, and answer feeding questions — take a written list of those questions with you.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Ask for a pet passport or health record, note the microchip number, and keep all documents in one place: you will need them for travel, grooming, insurance, and later for any specialist consultation.{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Conclusion{% endblocktranslate %}

{% blocktranslate trimmed %}Puppyhood is a short but extremely important developmental stage. Stable routines, gradual socialization, patience, and a safe environment help create a more confident and adaptable adult dog.{% endblocktranslate %}
